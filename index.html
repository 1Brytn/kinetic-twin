      White paper and product blueprint, October 2026

      The dashboard is dead. The warehouse is alive.

      A blueprint for a conversational digital twin WMS: a live spatial model of the distribution center that an agentic AI can see, reason over, simulate against, and act on, with a human approving every move that matters.

      1The paradigm shift

      For thirty years, warehouse software has asked people to do the hardest part of the job themselves. The WMS records that Truck 2481 is unloading. The yard system records that three trailers are waiting. The labor module records that forklift utilization dropped to 61%. The order pool records that a flash-sale wave just landed. Each fact is true. None of them is the answer. The supervisor is the integration layer, alt-tabbing between screens, phoning the dock lead, and stitching causality together in their head while the cutoff clock runs.

      Dashboards made this worse by making it look solved. A chart of dock utilization is a photograph of a river: accurate, attractive, and already out of date. It tells you what happened, rarely why, and never what to do next. Operations do not fail in a single metric. They fail in the interaction between metrics, in the queue that forms because a battery died in aisle C, which starved putaway, which filled staging, which stalled door D2, which left a late trailer in the yard, which carries the SKUs your e-commerce wave is about to short.

      The next generation of warehouse technology replaces the dashboard with a living model of the business: a spatially synchronized digital twin where every truck, door, pallet, aisle and forklift exists as a stateful object in real time, and an agentic AI that understands that model well enough to answer questions in plain language, trace the causal chain, simulate the alternatives, and propose the fix on the map. Operators stop reading the warehouse. They talk to it.

      The measure that matters shifts accordingly, from data freshness to decision latency: the time between a disruption forming and a correct, approved intervention executing on the floor. Traditional stacks measure this in tens of minutes. A conversational twin measures it in seconds.

      2High-level system architecture

      The platform is six layers joined by one principle: every event resolves to an entity, a location and a timestamp. That triple is what lets a sentence, a sensor reading and a pixel on the map refer to the same thing.

      Layer 1: Physical sensing at the edge

      • Real-time location: UWB RTLS tags on forklifts, pallet jacks and roll cages (sub-30 cm accuracy, 1 to 10 Hz), BLE for lower-value assets, RFID portals at dock doors to confirm pallet identity on crossing.
      • Machine telemetry: forklift CAN-bus data (load weight, mast height, battery state of charge, impact events) over 5G or private LTE.
      • Computer vision: door cameras that classify trailer presence, count pallets crossing the threshold, and detect blocked lanes or spills. Inference runs on edge GPUs so video never leaves the site.
      • Yard and transport: gate ANPR, yard-slot sensors, and carrier ELD/GPS feeds that turn appointment times into continuously updated ETAs.
      • Time discipline: PTP clock sync across gateways. Spatial synchronization is worthless if two sensors disagree about when something happened.

      Layer 2: Event backbone

      • Edge gateways normalize to MQTT with Sparkplug B payloads and bridge into a Kafka (or Redpanda) event log with an enforced schema registry.
      • The existing WMS, ERP and TMS are not replaced. Change-data-capture (Debezium) streams their transactional tables (ASNs, tasks, locations, orders) into the same log, so book state and physical state share one timeline.
      • Flink jobs derive operational signals continuously: dwell time per zone, travel-time inflation per aisle segment, door cycle time, throughput velocity, queue length and arrival rate.

      Layer 3: The twin state

      • Temporal knowledge graph holding the ontology (aligned to ISA-95 and Asset Administration Shell concepts): Site, Zone, Aisle, Location, Door, Trailer, ASN, Order, SKU, Pallet, Forklift, Operator, Task, and their relations (CARRIES, ASSIGNED_TO, LOCATED_IN, BLOCKS, FEEDS). Every edge is versioned in time, so the agent can ask what the graph looked like 40 minutes ago.
      • Spatial index generated from the facility CAD/BIM (IFC or OpenUSD): a navigation graph of aisles, intersections and doors, plus a grid of occupancy cells. Live congestion becomes edge weights.
      • Time-series store (TimescaleDB or similar) for high-frequency telemetry and derived KPIs.
      • State reconciler that continuously compares what the WMS believes against what sensors observe and raises divergence events: phantom inventory, unconfirmed putaways, a forklift the WMS thinks is working that has not moved in 12 minutes.

      Layer 4: Simulation and optimization

      • A discrete-event simulation engine that can fork the live state and run the next two to four hours forward in seconds, many times in parallel, one fork per candidate intervention.
      • Optimization services (OR-Tools, Gurobi) for door assignment, task interleaving, labor reallocation and dynamic slotting, exposed as callable tools rather than nightly batch jobs.
      • Forecasting models for carrier ETA, pick velocity by SKU and hour, and door cycle time, feeding both the simulator and the agent.

      Layer 5: Agentic AI

      • An orchestrator LLM agent that owns the conversation and plans, delegating to specialist agents: Yard and Dock, Inventory and Slotting, Fleet and Labor, Outbound and OTIF, and a Simulation agent.
      • A governed tool layer exposed over MCP: graph.traverse, metrics.query (against a semantic metric layer, never raw production SQL), spatial.path, spatial.density, sim.fork_and_run, opt.solve, action.propose.
      • Retrieval over SOPs, carrier contracts, SLA and detention terms, and safety rules, so recommendations respect how the site actually runs.
      • A policy engine (for example OPA) that decides which actions the agent may only suggest, which need a named approver, and which it may execute inside tight guardrails. Everything is logged for audit.

      Layer 6: Experience and write-back

      • A 2D vector twin for web and mobile, and a 3D twin (WebGL/Three.js, or Omniverse/Unreal pixel streaming for control rooms), both rendering the same entity IDs.
      • The copilot returns structured output alongside its prose: entity IDs to highlight, geometry to draw, and typed actions with predicted impact. The UI binds all three to the map.
      • Approved actions are written back to the WMS and to driver tablets through its APIs using a transactional outbox, so the twin never edits the system of record directly.

      3The conversational and agentic logic

      From a sentence to a grounded answer

      When an operator asks "Why is shipment #78466 delayed?", the agent runs a fixed loop:

      1. Entity resolution. "#78466" is matched in the graph to an ASN, the trailer carrying it, the carrier, the appointment, and its current location (yard slot 3). Ambiguity is resolved by asking, never by guessing.
      2. Planning. The orchestrator decomposes the question into tool calls: trailer timeline, door states, staging occupancy, putaway capacity, aisle travel-time inflation.
      3. Grounded retrieval. Each call returns typed data with provenance. The model never invents a number; every figure in the answer traces to a tool result.
      4. Causal reasoning. The agent walks the dependency graph backward from the symptom. Queueing theory does the heavy lifting: by Little's Law (L = λW), a rise in staging inventory with constant arrivals means service rate fell; wait time grows non-linearly as utilization passes about 85%, so a small capacity loss can explain a large delay.
      5. Spatial grounding. The answer carries the entity IDs of every link in the chain (the trailer, door D2, the staging lane, aisle C, forklift FL-02) so the twin lights up the story, not just the headline.
      6. Prescription. Candidate interventions are generated, each simulated in a forked twin, and scored on cost of delay, OTIF risk, detention fees, labor and safety.
      7. Approval and execution. The operator approves; the action is written back and tracked.
      8. Learning. Predicted versus actual outcomes are logged and used to recalibrate the simulator and the agent's ranking of interventions.

      Understanding space, not just tables

      The warehouse is modelled as a weighted navigation graph whose edge costs update from RTLS dwell and travel times. That gives the agent spatial tools a spreadsheet cannot: shortest feasible path for a forklift avoiding a blocked segment, the nearest idle resource to a door, density of vehicles in a zone, and the walking-distance impact of moving a SKU from aisle C to aisle D. "What is near door D2?" and "which aisle is choking putaway?" become graph queries with geometry attached.

      From reporting to prescribing

      • Constraint discovery: the agent identifies the binding constraint (theory of constraints) rather than listing every red metric. Usually there is one.
      • Counterfactual testing: "if we cross-dock trailer 2617 and move FL-04 to picking, what happens to the 14:00 cutoff?" is answered by simulation, not opinion.
      • Multi-agent negotiation: the Outbound agent wants every forklift on picking; the Yard agent wants doors cleared. The orchestrator resolves the trade-off against site objectives and shows its reasoning.
      • Graduated autonomy: level 0 inform, level 1 recommend, level 2 act with approval, level 3 act within guardrails with automatic rollback. Sites earn autonomy per action type, with evidence.

      4Real-world use cases

      Time and impact figures below are illustrative design targets for a mid-size DC, not measured results. Each scenario can be run in the live twin.

      The 14:00 collision: delayed inbound meets an e-commerce surge

      Three trailers arrive together, 45 to 70 minutes late. Minutes later a marketplace flash sale drops a wave with a tight carrier cutoff. Inbound staging is already above 80%.

      Traditional WMS

      The supervisor checks the yard list, the wave backlog and the labor screen separately. It takes 45 to 90 minutes, and a walk to the dock, to realise the late trailer carries the very SKUs the wave is short. The decision is made on instinct.

      Conversational twin

      "What breaks first if we keep the current plan?" The agent joins ASN contents to wave shortages, sees the overlap, simulates three options and recommends: prioritize the urgent trailer, cross-dock it straight to outbound staging, and re-balance one forklift. Decision in under 5 minutes.

      Dynamic forklift rerouting around a choking aisle

      A shrink-wrap spill and a cluster of fast movers turn aisle C into a parking lot. No alarm fires; trips just get slower.

      Traditional WMS

      Throughput sags for an hour before anyone notices the pattern in end-of-shift productivity reports. Drivers improvise routes. Slotting is reviewed next month.

      Conversational twin

      RTLS shows travel-time inflation on the aisle C segments within minutes. The agent flags it, reroutes task assignment around the aisle, and proposes dynamic re-slotting of the hottest SKUs into aisle D's golden zone.

      "Why is shipment #78466 late?" and the root cause nobody suspected

      Everyone blames the carrier. The carrier was late, but that is not what is holding the shipment.

      Traditional WMS

      The ASN shows "not received". The yard shows "waiting". A coordinator calls the carrier, then the dock, then maintenance. The real chain surfaces after the detention clock has started.

      Conversational twin

      The answer traces the chain on the map: trailer in yard, all inbound doors busy, D2 stalled because staging is full, staging full because putaway lost a forklift to a battery fault. It proposes a battery swap and a forklift reassignment, then prioritizes the trailer.

      5How to prove it works

      • Decision latency: disruption detected to approved action executing.
      • Dock-to-stock time and door turn time, plus trailer detention minutes.
      • Throughput velocity (pallet moves per hour) and its variance across the shift.
      • OTIF at carrier cutoff, especially on surge days.
      • Recommendation acceptance rate and predicted-versus-actual impact of accepted actions.

      Risks worth taking seriously

      • Sensor coverage and data quality. A twin built on gaps is a confident liar. Start with doors, forklifts and staging, where the signal-to-cost ratio is highest.
      • Hallucination. Mitigated by tool-only grounding, cited figures, and refusing to answer when data is missing.
      • Safety. The agent never moves people or machines directly; it proposes tasks through the existing WMS and safety rules.
      • Adoption. Supervisors trust what they can verify. Showing the chain on the map is the trust mechanism.

      The live twin in this artifact is a simulation with synthetic data. Inside Claude, its copilot answers with Claude, grounded in a snapshot of the simulated state; in the public demo it runs on a built-in rules engine over the same state.
